A business looks at a questionnaire and sees the data it needs.

A customer looks at the same questionnaire and sees a series of small decisions. *Should I answer this? Why do they want to know? How much more will they ask?*

At audibene, that difference mattered more than in most businesses. People with hearing loss often postpone the decision for a long time. When they finally start an online questionnaire, their motivation is real, but fragile. One uncomfortable question too early, and they are gone.

So instead of asking how short the questionnaire could be, we asked a different question: **in what order should the questions come?**

We redesigned the sequence around progressive commitment:

- **Start with questions anyone can answer** without thinking, and without feeling exposed.
- **Move gradually toward the personal and demanding ones**, once the customer has already invested a few answers.
- **Make every step feel like progress**, so the next question feels like the natural continuation of a conversation, not a new hurdle.

The redesigned sequence won the test.

The insight stayed with me long after Berlin. Years later, I used the same principle to rebuild the lead forms of a luxury property developer in Ho Chi Minh City, and it helped triple leads while cutting cost per lead by about 75%. [Read that story.](/stories/the-lead-form-that-asks-the-easy-question-first/)

**The lesson:** Designing a form is not about removing fields. It is about understanding what it feels like to be asked.
