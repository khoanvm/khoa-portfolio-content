Most career case studies are about solving a problem for a company or a customer. This one is different. It is about two kinds of work I chose to pursue because I wanted to become better at my profession: **teaching what I know and building tools for problems I experience myself.**

Neither began as a conventional commercial project. Both became practical ways to test my knowledge, strengthen my communication, and develop a more credible professional identity.

## The starting point: experience is not the same as understanding

Working across experimentation, marketing, sales, and partnerships gave me a range of experiences. But experience alone does not guarantee that I can explain a decision clearly, transfer a method to someone else, or apply a new technology responsibly.

I wanted to challenge myself beyond the daily demands of a job. Teaching and building offered two complementary forms of feedback: **Can I make an idea understandable to another person? And can I turn that idea into something that works?**

## Teaching: learning to make complex ideas useful

I have taught Online Marketing at the Digital Career Institute in Berlin and contributed as a guest lecturer in MBA Digital Marketing.

Teaching pushed me to revisit concepts I had used in practice: customer behavior, experimentation, acquisition, measurement, and commercial decision-making. Explaining a framework to learners is very different from using it as shorthand with colleagues.

A good session requires me to organize an argument, choose relevant examples, make assumptions explicit, respond to questions, and recognize when an explanation is not landing. It also requires listening. Learners do not necessarily share the same background or vocabulary.

Over time, I came to see teaching as a way to improve capabilities that matter well beyond a classroom: presenting a recommendation, aligning a cross-functional team, discussing trade-offs, and helping someone act on an idea independently.

**The value is not simply standing in front of an audience. It is making knowledge transferable.**

## Building with AI: learning by confronting real constraints

Alongside teaching, I began developing two personal workflow products: **Khoa Video Studio** and **Khoa Content Studio**.

The Video Studio explores how voice-led content can move through transcription, storyboarding, scene editing, preview, and video export. The Content Studio organizes idea discovery, source material, writing, and revision history into a more deliberate editorial workflow.

I approached these projects as a product owner and hands-on, AI-assisted builder. That meant defining the user problem, mapping the workflow, specifying requirements, evaluating models, working through implementation decisions, testing behavior, and deciding what was acceptable to release.

The process taught me as much about my own assumptions as it did about technology. A convincing demonstration is not the same as a dependable workflow. Integration, error handling, cost, human review, and usability matter after the first output looks impressive.

Some components are operational; others remain experimental. These are independent learning and product initiatives, **not finished commercial SaaS products or evidence of revenue generated by AI**. The detailed technical journey is documented separately in my [AI product case study](/case-studies/ai-product-initiatives/).

## Professional visibility: earning credibility through useful work

There is also a professional reason I value these activities. In an international career, a job title or résumé can only explain so much about how someone thinks, communicates, and learns.

Teaching gives others a chance to experience how I explain ideas and engage with questions. Building gives me concrete work to discuss: design decisions, prototypes, tests, mistakes, and improvements. Writing about that work helps me reflect on what I actually learned.

Together, these activities help me create a professional presence grounded in substance rather than polished claims. **For me, professional visibility should be a consequence of doing, explaining, and sharing useful work — not the goal that replaces the work.**

## Outcomes so far

I can point to two ongoing AI product initiatives and two distinct teaching settings. More importantly, I have built habits I can carry into business roles: explaining complex topics in plain language, making decisions visible, learning from feedback, and bridging commercial needs with technical possibilities.

I do not attach an invented audience-growth number, teaching impact score, or financial return to these activities. Their current value is in the capabilities developed and the work that can be examined.

## What I learned

**Teaching exposes gaps in understanding. Building exposes gaps in execution. Sharing both exposes my thinking to questions and feedback.**

That combination is a form of professional development I intend to keep practicing. It makes me a more thoughtful learner, a clearer communicator, and a more useful collaborator — while allowing my professional reputation to grow from evidence of the work itself.
